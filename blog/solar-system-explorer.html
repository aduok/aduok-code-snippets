Terrestrial planet

Mercury

The smallest planet and the closest to the Sun, Mercury races around our star in just 88 days — a speed that earned it the name of the Roman messenger of the gods.

  1. Wrinkled and shrinking

    As its iron core cooled, Mercury contracted and its crust crumpled into cliffs called lobate scarps, some over a kilometre high.

  2. Two years, one day

    One solar day — sunrise to sunrise — lasts 176 Earth days, twice as long as the planet's 88-day year.

  3. Extreme swings

    With almost no atmosphere to hold heat, the surface falls to about −180 °C at night and climbs to roughly 430 °C by day.

  1. Hotter than Mercury

    A thick carbon-dioxide atmosphere traps heat so well that the surface is hot enough to melt lead.

  2. Backwards and slow

    Venus spins in the opposite direction to most planets, taking 243 Earth days to turn once — longer than its year.

  3. Crushing pressure

    Air pressure at the surface is about 92 times Earth's, similar to being roughly 900 metres under the ocean.

  1. An ocean world

    About 71% of the surface is covered in water, which is why Earth looks blue from space.

  2. A magnetic shield

    Swirling molten iron and nickel in the core generate a magnetic field that deflects most of the solar wind.

  3. Steadied by the Moon

    The Moon's gravity helps stabilise Earth's tilt, giving us regular seasons. It is also drifting away by about 3.8 cm every year.

  1. The Great Red Spot

    This storm is wider than Earth and has been observed for more than 150 years, although it has been slowly shrinking.

  2. The shortest day

    Jupiter spins once every 9 hours 56 minutes, so fast that the planet bulges at the equator.

  3. Galileo's moons

    In 1610 Galileo spotted Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto — the first moons seen orbiting another world. Europa hides a salty ocean beneath its ice.

  1. Found by mathematics

    Wobbles in Uranus's orbit led Urbain Le Verrier to predict a new planet; Johann Galle found it in 1846 within a degree of the predicted spot.

  2. The fastest winds

    Winds reach about 2,000 km/h, the fastest measured on any planet in the solar system.

  3. A captured moon

    Triton orbits backwards, which suggests it was captured from the Kuiper Belt. Voyager 2 spotted geysers erupting from its icy surface.

Pluto

  1. A heart of ice

    NASA's New Horizons flyby in 2015 revealed Tombaugh Regio, a vast heart-shaped plain of nitrogen ice.

  2. A double world

    Charon is half of Pluto's diameter, and the two orbit a point in the space between them, always showing each other the same face.

  3. Very slow seasons

    Discovered in 1930 by Clyde Tombaugh, Pluto has not yet completed a single orbit since — each one takes 248 years.
